Project Snapshot

  • Role: Mechatronics, Firmware, and Testing (Group Project)
  • Hardware Stack: Distributed ESP32 Architecture (Knee & Shoe Units), BNO055 IMUs, I2C
  • Software Stack: C++, FreeRTOS, LittleFS, MQTT, Node-RED, InfluxDB, Grafana
  • Key Outcome: Co-developed a £133 clinical knee monitor (180% cheaper than market alternatives). Spearheaded the mechatronics and executed a solo software overhaul post-testing, delivering a dual-core RTOS architecture with zero-data-loss network resilience.

Team Hardware Design: A Distributed System

Osteoarthritis (OA) and post-Total Knee Replacement (TKR) patients require long-term gait monitoring, but commercial instrumented sleeves cost upwards of £375. As a group, we divided the research to design a highly affordable, non-invasive alternative.

Instead of routing all sensors to a bulky central hub, the team designed a distributed embedded system featuring two distinct nodes: a Knee Unit and a Shoe Unit, each powered by its own ESP32 microcontroller and independent battery pack. Three Adafruit BNO055 IMUs were distributed across the thigh, shin, and shoe.

Within this architecture, I took primary ownership of the Knee Unit mechatronics. I wired and integrated the thigh and shin IMUs via I2C to the knee's localized ESP32 to capture complex Euler angles for real-time knee flexion calculation.

Team IoT Architecture & Telemetry

To enable remote clinical observation, the team engineered an end-to-end telemetry pipeline.

  • Transmission & Routing: A teammate led the setup of the wireless transmission, establishing a lightweight MQTT protocol broadcast that a Node-RED bridge routed directly into a time-series InfluxDB database.
  • Visualization: This pipeline successfully fed a custom, web-based Grafana dashboard displaying knee flexion angles, stride length, and cadence in real-time.

Clinical Validation Testing (My Role)

I was heavily responsible for the system's testing and validation. I led the evaluation against gold-standard Vicon Nexus optical tracking in a clinical gait laboratory. While the general gait curve was captured, my testing revealed critical system flaws:

  • Flexion Accuracy: The system showed a -13.94° mean bias and a 17.50° RMSE.
  • The Timing Bottleneck: Spatiotemporal metrics (like cadence and stride length) exhibited massive errors (>20%). Through rigorous debugging, I identified that the linear Arduino code loop was the culprit. Even though I didn't build the original network pipeline, I deeply understood its architecture, allowing me to diagnose that the blocking nature of the Wi-Fi/MQTT transmission was causing system latency, delaying the IMU sensor polling and ruining the time-series accuracy.

Software Fix: RTOS & Network Resilience

Following the clinical tests, I took solo ownership of the codebase to eliminate the network-blocking latency and ensure true clinical reliability. I completely re-architected the Knee Unit's firmware using FreeRTOS and LittleFS.

  • Task Decoupling: Pinned the high-speed IMU sensor polling (50Hz) and "medical zeroing" mathematics entirely to Core 1. The volatile Wi-Fi/MQTT connection management was isolated to Core 0, ensuring network drops never delay physical sensor reads.
  • Zero-Data-Loss Offline Storage: Engineered a non-blocking reconnect mechanism on Core 0. If the clinical network drops, the system seamlessly redirects the 50Hz sensor stream to the ESP32's internal flash memory to prevent data loss. Upon reconnection, it automatically bulk-publishes the offline backlog to the MQTT broker.
  • Real-Time Safety Alerts: Added a concurrent safety monitor task on Core 1 that triggers an immediate physical LED warning if the patient exceeds a dangerous 120° medical flexion threshold (e.g., performing a deep squat).
