Project

Low-Cost Smart Insole

Engineered a lightweight, sub-£75 wearable insole prototype for clinical gait analysis and ML-driven activity monitoring.

View repo

Project Snapshot

  • Hardware Stack: TinyDuino (C++), Interlink FSRs, CAD (Fusion360), FARO 3D Scanning
  • Software Stack: Python, Random Forest, Scikit-Learn, Vicon Nexus
  • Key Outcome: Prototyped a £72.50 wearable gait analysis insole that preserves natural biomechanics, integrating an automated ML pipeline for activity classification.

The Challenge & Mechanical Design

Gold-standard instrumented insoles cost upwards of £2,000, severely limiting clinical accessibility for children with physical disabilities. The objective was to engineer an unobtrusive, wearable alternative using off-the-shelf components.

To maximize wearer comfort and hide the compute unit, I utilized a FARO Quantum Max ScanArm to laser-scan consumer insoles. I then designed a custom PLA electronics enclosure to fit securely within a carved cavity in the heel, bypassing the latent elasticity issues found in early 3D-printed TPU prototypes.

Mechatronics Architecture

The system's intelligence relies on the modular TinyDuino platform. I integrated three Interlink 406 Force-Sensitive Resistors (FSRs) at high-pressure zones (medial, lateral, and heel).

Analog voltage shifts caused by the piezoelectric effect are processed by the microcontroller and continuously logged to an onboard MicroSD shield at 14 Hz. This created a completely untethered data-logging environment for real-world movement tracking.

Clinical Validation & Hardware Insights

Before advancing to physical activity classification, the hardware was rigorously tested in a clinical gait laboratory to establish baseline accuracy.

  • Biomechanical Impact (Vicon Nexus): Tracked via optical motion capture, the insole proved highly non-disruptive to natural gait, yielding a negligible 1.73% NRMSE in knee angle deviation compared to uninstrumented shoes.
  • Temporal Accuracy (Force Plates): Compared against 960Hz force plates, the relative pressure trends were reliably preserved. However, the system overestimated Ground Contact Time (GCT) by 7.98%. This provided a crucial engineering insight: the physical hysteresis inherent in FSRs requires adaptive software thresholding.

Machine Learning Activity Classification

To move beyond manual, rule-based thresholding, I engineered an automated ML pipeline using the 14 Hz pressure and motion data stream to classify specific physical activities.

  • Data Prep & Extraction: Synchronized raw sensor data with video ground truth, extracting 67-dimensional feature vectors from 2-second (guarentee to capture full gait cycle) sliding windows.
  • Model Training: Evaluated Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and SVM classifiers using rigorous Leave-One-Group-Out cross-validation to prevent data leakage.
  • Results & Limitations: The Random Forest ensemble won, classifying high-frequency activities like walking with 97% accuracy. However, activities with insufficient training samples (like stair climbing) failed to generalize, highlighting the need for multi-subject datasets before real-world clinical deployment.
